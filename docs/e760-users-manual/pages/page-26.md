All patients should discuss with their physician their treatment frequency (number of
treatments per week). The common choices are listed in the exposure guideline tables. Consider
taking treatments using lowest effective number of times per week to reduce your total lifetime
cumulative ultraviolet dose. Never increase exposure times on consecutive days. The optimal
frequency of UVB phototherapy treatments has not been established.

General Exposure Information:
1. Long term use of this system must be accompanied by physician examination (skin check) at
    least once per year; this is a condition of sale. See your physician if you have not achieved
    promising results after 2 to 4 months of diligent treatments.
2. The maximum dosage is generally determined by the slightest onset of any mild pinkness of the
    skin - in the healthy skin for psoriasis and eczema, and in the white lesions for vitiligo. This is
    commonly referred to as “sub-erythema” (just below the state of erythema or sunburn). This
    produces a slight "warm" feeling on the skin but is not a sunburn.
    UVB phototherapy does not require that you ever become red due to sunburn.
    In fact, burning may worsen your skin problem. The various degrees of erythema and its
    associated discomfort are inherent risks in UVB phototherapy. Dosages less than the maximum
    can have positive therapeutic results and may be the safest approach to long term use of
    the system.
3. There is a latency period of 3 to 24 hours before maximum reddening of the skin. Under no
    circumstances should treatments be repeated on the same area within 24 hours. Some
    patients may experience latency periods of up to 48 hours and should therefore be treated no
    more than every second day. Some medications may extend the latency period up to 72 hours.
4. Consider taking a shower or bath immediately before your UVB treatment, ideally using only
    residue-free/fragrance-free soap and shampoo. There are three reasons for this: a) dead skin
    partially blocks UVB light, b) wet skin has better UVB transmission properties than dry skin, and
    c) having clean skin reduces the possibility of harmful UVB light reactions with foreign substances
    lying in or on the skin. Those with thick psoriasis lesions may wish to assist the exfoliation
    process further by gently rubbing away the dead skin once it has softened.
5. Because UVB light is a natural part of the sun's spectrum, treat any exposures to natural sunlight
    as you would the UVB light from your SolRx system. Skip your home UVB treatment if any part of
    your body has received significant amounts of natural sunlight.
6. Do not use a tanning bed or a solarium at any time during the course of your treatments.
7. Many perfumes, medicated soaps and creams can cause adverse reactions of the skin when
    combined with UVB light. If hives, blisters, or red blotches appear, discontinue your UVB
    treatments and consult your physician. Isolate the offending agent and take action accordingly.
8. Many herbal remedies and common foods can increase your sensitivity to the ultraviolet light;
    such as, but not limited to: St.John’s Wort, celery, parsley, rosemary, motherwort, wild carrot and
    aniseed. Consult your physician if you are using these substances.
9. Do not take any new medications; oral, by needle, or topical (applied to the skin directly); by
    prescription or otherwise, without consulting your physician about the effects on your
    phototherapy treatments.
10. For some skin diseases, the use of some medications; oral, by needle, or topical (applied to the
    skin directly, such as mineral oil); by prescription or otherwise, may increase the effectiveness of
    your UVB phototherapy, but their use is entirely on the advice of your physician and is beyond the
    scope of this User’s Manual. Note that some topical medications, such as Calcipotriol (Dovonex,
    Dovobet, Taclonex), are inactivated by UVB light and are partially UVB blocking; so they should
    be applied only after UVB treatment.
